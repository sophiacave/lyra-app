<div class="lesson-section">
  <span class="section-label">Ages 5-8</span>
  <h2 class="section-title">Early learners: supervised exploration.</h2>
  <p>At this age, AI should feel like a toy, not a tool. Your child doesn't need a chatbot — they need interactive experiences with guardrails built in.</p>
  <p><strong>Good fits:</strong> Voice assistants with kid modes (Amazon Kids, Google Kids Space), AI-powered reading apps like Epic! or Ello, and creative tools like Google's Quick Draw or AutoDraw. These tools have content filters, limited data collection, and are designed for young minds.</p>
  <p><strong>What to avoid:</strong> Open-ended chatbots (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini) without supervision. These tools aren't designed for young children, and the outputs can be unpredictable.</p>
  <div class="tip-box">
    <strong>Rule of thumb for this age:</strong> If it requires reading complex outputs or making judgment calls about accuracy, your child isn't ready to use it independently.
  </div>
</div>

<div class="lesson-section">
  <span class="section-label">Ages 9-12</span>
  <h2 class="section-title">Middle schoolers: guided independence.</h2>
  <p>This is where it gets interesting. Kids this age are curious, capable, and starting to do real academic work. AI can genuinely help — but they need a framework for using it responsibly.</p>
  <p><strong>Good fits:</strong> Khan Academy's Khanmigo (built specifically for students with guardrails), Duolingo (AI-powered language learning), Scratch with AI extensions (coding + creativity), and AI art tools like Canva's kid-friendly features.</p>
  <p><strong>Supervised use:</strong> ChatGPT or Claude with a parent's account, sitting together. This is a great age to start showing them how to prompt well and how to fact-check AI outputs.</p>
  <div class="callout">
    <strong>The golden rule:</strong> At this age, teach them to use AI as a thinking partner, not an answer machine. "Ask it to explain, not to do" is a phrase worth repeating.
  </div>
</div>

<div class="lesson-section">
  <span class="section-label">Ages 13-17</span>
  <h2 class="section-title">Teens: structured autonomy.</h2>
  <p>Your teenager is going to use AI whether you set it up or not. Every classmate has access. The question isn't whether — it's how well they use it.</p>
  <p><strong>Good fits:</strong> ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini (with their own accounts where age-appropriate), GitHub Copilot for teens learning to code, Grammarly for writing support, and Notion AI for organizing schoolwork.</p>
  <p><strong>Key conversations:</strong> Academic integrity (what counts as cheating at their school), data privacy (what they share with AI tools stays somewhere), and critical evaluation (AI can be confidently wrong).</p>
  <p><strong>The handoff:</strong> By 16-17, your role shifts from gatekeeper to advisor. You're not controlling access anymore — you're building judgment. If you've done the earlier stages well, they'll have the critical thinking skills to navigate this.</p>
</div>

<div class="lesson-section">
  <span class="section-label">Evaluation Framework</span>
  <h2 class="section-title">How to vet any new AI tool.</h2>
  <p>New AI tools launch constantly. Here's your five-point checklist for evaluating any tool before your kid uses it:</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Privacy policy:</strong> Does it collect data from minors? Is it COPPA compliant?</li>
    <li><strong>Content filters:</strong> Can it generate inappropriate content? Are there safety rails?</li>
    <li><strong>Age rating:</strong> What age does the company recommend? (Check the app store AND the company's terms of service.)</li>
    <li><strong>Output quality:</strong> Try it yourself first. Ask it things your kid would ask. See what comes back.</li>
    <li><strong>Business model:</strong> If it's free, your child's data might be the product. Paid tools with clear privacy policies are often safer.</li>
  </ul>
  <div class="tip-box">
    <strong>Pro move:</strong> Spend 15 minutes with any new tool before your kid touches it. You'll catch 90% of issues just by playing with it yourself.
  </div>
</div>
