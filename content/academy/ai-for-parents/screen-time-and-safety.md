<div class="lesson-section">
  <span class="section-label">Rethinking Screen Time</span>
  <h2 class="section-title">Not all screen time is equal — and AI proves it.</h2>
  <p>The old "two hours of screen time" rule was designed for passive consumption — watching TV, scrolling social media. But a kid using AI to learn coding, write stories, or explore science isn't doing the same thing as a kid watching TikTok for three hours.</p>
  <p>This doesn't mean AI gets unlimited time. It means you need a more nuanced framework:</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Passive consumption</strong> (watching, scrolling) — limit this aggressively.</li>
    <li><strong>Active creation</strong> (using AI to build, write, code, create) — more flexible, but still needs boundaries.</li>
    <li><strong>Interactive learning</strong> (AI tutoring, language practice) — most flexible, but watch for fatigue.</li>
  </ul>
  <div class="tip-box">
    <strong>The quality question:</strong> Instead of "how long were you on a screen?" try asking "what did you make or learn?" This shifts the conversation from time policing to value assessment.
  </div>
</div>

<div class="lesson-section">
  <span class="section-label">Privacy Reality Check</span>
  <h2 class="section-title">What AI tools know about your kid.</h2>
  <p>Every conversation your child has with an AI tool is potentially stored, analyzed, and used to train future models. That's not a scare tactic — it's the business model. Here's what typically gets collected:</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Conversation content:</strong> Everything they type or say. Full transcripts.</li>
    <li><strong>Usage patterns:</strong> When they use it, how often, what topics they explore.</li>
    <li><strong>Device information:</strong> Location, device type, browser, IP address.</li>
    <li><strong>Account details:</strong> Email, name, age (if they provided it truthfully).</li>
  </ul>
  <p>For kids under 13, COPPA (Children's Online Privacy Protection Act) requires parental consent before collecting personal data. But enforcement is imperfect, and many kids use adult accounts or lie about their age.</p>
  <div class="callout">
    <strong>The rule:</strong> Never share personal information with AI. No full names, addresses, school names, phone numbers, or photos of your child. Treat every AI conversation as if it's being posted publicly — because in some ways, it is.
  </div>
</div>

<div class="lesson-section">
  <span class="section-label">Settings to Configure</span>
  <h2 class="section-title">Your privacy checklist — do this today.</h2>
  <p>Most AI tools have privacy settings buried in menus. Here's what to find and adjust:</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Chat history:</strong> Turn off chat history saving where possible. In ChatGPT: Settings > Data Controls > Chat History & Training.</li>
    <li><strong>Training opt-out:</strong> Many tools let you opt out of your data being used to train models. Always opt out for children's accounts.</li>
    <li><strong>Shared links:</strong> Disable the ability to share conversations publicly.</li>
    <li><strong>Connected apps:</strong> Review what third-party apps have access to your child's AI accounts.</li>
    <li><strong>Content filters:</strong> Enable maximum safety filters on any AI tool your child uses.</li>
  </ul>
</div>

<div class="lesson-section">
  <span class="section-label">Boundaries That Work</span>
  <h2 class="section-title">Rules your kids will actually follow.</h2>
  <p>Strict bans don't work. They just teach kids to hide their usage. Instead, try agreements:</p>
  <p><strong>The Family AI Agreement:</strong></p>
  <ol>
    <li>AI is used in common spaces (not behind closed doors) until trust is established.</li>
    <li>No personal information goes into AI — ever.</li>
    <li>Homework AI use follows the 3-step flow (attempt, assist, redo).</li>
    <li>If AI generates something confusing or scary, come talk to a parent. No trouble.</li>
    <li>We review AI usage together once a month — not to punish, but to learn together.</li>
  </ol>
  <div class="tip-box">
    <strong>Make it collaborative:</strong> Let your kids help write the agreement. Rules they helped create are rules they'll actually follow.
  </div>
</div>
