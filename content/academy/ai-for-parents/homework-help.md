<div class="lesson-section">
  <span class="section-label">The Line</span>
  <h2 class="section-title">There's a clear difference — and your kid knows it.</h2>
  <p>Let's be honest: your child already knows they can paste a homework question into ChatGPT and get an answer. That ship has sailed. So the question isn't about preventing it — it's about teaching them why copying an AI answer is like having someone else do their push-ups. You don't get stronger.</p>
  <p><strong>AI as tutor:</strong> "I don't understand why this fraction equals that. Can you explain it step by step?" — This is learning.</p>
  <p><strong>AI as cheat code:</strong> "Solve problems 1-20 on page 47." — This is avoidance.</p>
  <p>The distinction is simple: if your child understands the material better after using AI, it worked. If they just have answers on paper, it didn't.</p>
</div>

<div class="lesson-section">
  <span class="section-label">Prompting for Learning</span>
  <h2 class="section-title">Teach your kids these prompts.</h2>
  <p>The way you ask AI determines what you get. These prompting patterns turn AI from an answer machine into a personal tutor:</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>"Explain this like I'm [age]"</strong> — Gets explanations at the right level.</li>
    <li><strong>"Don't give me the answer. Ask me questions to help me figure it out."</strong> — Socratic method. This is gold.</li>
    <li><strong>"I think the answer is X. Am I right? Why or why not?"</strong> — Builds reasoning, not dependency.</li>
    <li><strong>"Give me a similar practice problem"</strong> — Practice without copying.</li>
    <li><strong>"What concept do I need to understand to solve this?"</strong> — Identifies knowledge gaps.</li>
  </ul>
  <div class="tip-box">
    <strong>Print these out.</strong> Seriously. Tape them next to the computer. When these prompts become habit, AI becomes the best tutor your kid has ever had — available 24/7, infinitely patient, and always at their level.
  </div>
</div>

<div class="lesson-section">
  <span class="section-label">Setting Up Sessions</span>
  <h2 class="section-title">Structure matters more than surveillance.</h2>
  <p>You don't need to hover over your child's shoulder. You need a simple structure:</p>
  <p><strong>The 3-step homework flow:</strong></p>
  <ol>
    <li><strong>Attempt first.</strong> Your child tries the work on their own. They mark what they're stuck on.</li>
    <li><strong>AI assist.</strong> For the stuck parts only, they use AI with the learning prompts above. AI explains — it doesn't solve.</li>
    <li><strong>Redo.</strong> They go back and complete the stuck problems themselves, using what they learned.</li>
  </ol>
  <p>This flow takes maybe 10 extra minutes. But it's the difference between a child who learns and a child who has a pile of AI-generated answers they can't explain.</p>
  <div class="callout">
    <strong>The explain-back test:</strong> Ask your child to explain their homework to you without looking at it. If they can, they learned. If they can't, the AI did the work. This one check catches everything.
  </div>
</div>

<div class="lesson-section">
  <span class="section-label">Subject-Specific Tips</span>
  <h2 class="section-title">Different subjects, different rules.</h2>
  <p><strong>Math:</strong> AI is excellent at step-by-step explanations. Have it show work, then your child practices similar problems independently. Never paste entire problem sets.</p>
  <p><strong>Writing:</strong> This is where it gets tricky. AI can brainstorm ideas, help outline, check grammar, and give feedback on drafts. But the actual writing must be your child's voice. A good test: if your kid couldn't write that sentence in conversation, it shouldn't be in their paper.</p>
  <p><strong>Science:</strong> AI shines at explaining concepts and helping with research. It's less reliable for specific data and citations — teach your kids to verify facts from AI against textbooks or reputable sources.</p>
  <p><strong>History & Social Studies:</strong> Great for getting multiple perspectives and understanding context. But AI can hallucinate dates, names, and events. Always cross-reference with course materials.</p>
</div>
