<div class="lesson-section">
  <span class="section-label">The Core Problem</span>
  <h2 class="section-title">AI sounds right even when it's wrong.</h2>
  <p>This is the single most important thing to teach your child about AI: it generates text that sounds authoritative, polished, and certain — even when it's completely made up. AI doesn't know what's true. It knows what sounds true based on patterns. Those are very different things.</p>
  <p>AI "hallucinations" — when AI invents facts, citations, or events — don't come with warning labels. There's no red text or alarm bell. The made-up fact sits right next to real facts in the same confident tone. Adults fall for this constantly. Kids are even more vulnerable.</p>
  <div class="callout">
    <strong>The mantra:</strong> "AI is a confident intern, not an expert." It does great work most of the time. But you always have to check its work because it will never tell you when it's guessing.
  </div>
</div>

<div class="lesson-section">
  <span class="section-label">The Exercises</span>
  <h2 class="section-title">Three games that build critical thinking.</h2>
  <p><strong>1. Fact or Fabrication (ages 8+):</strong> Ask AI a question you already know the answer to. Have your child identify which parts of the answer are correct and which might be wrong. Start with topics they know well — their favorite sport, a book they've read, their hometown.</p>
  <p><strong>2. The Source Challenge (ages 10+):</strong> Give your child an AI-generated paragraph about a historical event. Their job: find a real source (book, encyclopedia, reputable website) that confirms or contradicts each claim. Keep score — how accurate was AI?</p>
  <p><strong>3. Bias Detective (ages 12+):</strong> Ask AI the same question three different ways and compare the answers. "Was Columbus a hero?" vs. "Was Columbus a villain?" vs. "What did Columbus do?" Show your child how framing changes the output — and discuss why.</p>
  <div class="tip-box">
    <strong>Make it fun:</strong> These aren't chores. Frame them as detective work. Kids love catching AI making mistakes — it gives them a sense of power and mastery over the technology.
  </div>
</div>

<div class="lesson-section">
  <span class="section-label">Daily Habits</span>
  <h2 class="section-title">Build verification into the routine.</h2>
  <p>Critical thinking isn't a one-time lesson. It's a habit you build through repetition. Here are three habits that stick:</p>
  <p><strong>The Two-Source Rule:</strong> If AI tells you something important, verify it with at least one other source before you believe it. This applies to homework, research, and anything that matters.</p>
  <p><strong>The "How Do You Know?" Question:</strong> When your child shares something they learned from AI, ask "How do you know that's true?" Not accusingly — curiously. Normalize the idea that knowing where information comes from matters as much as the information itself.</p>
  <p><strong>The Confidence Check:</strong> Teach your child to ask AI: "How confident are you in this answer? What might you be wrong about?" AI tools often give more nuanced responses when directly asked about their limitations.</p>
</div>

<div class="lesson-section">
  <span class="section-label">Beyond AI</span>
  <h2 class="section-title">This skill protects them everywhere.</h2>
  <p>Here's the beautiful thing about teaching AI critical thinking: it transfers to everything. A kid who questions AI outputs will also question social media claims, political ads, viral misinformation, and too-good-to-be-true scams.</p>
  <p>You're not just teaching them about technology. You're teaching them about epistemology — how we know what we know. That's one of the most valuable intellectual skills a human being can have, and AI just gave you the perfect classroom for teaching it.</p>
</div>
