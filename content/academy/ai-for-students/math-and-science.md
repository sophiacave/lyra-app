<div class="lesson-section">
  <span class="section-label">The Trap</span>
  <h2 class="section-title">Getting the answer is easy. Understanding why it's the answer is the whole point.</h2>
  <p class="section-text">You can paste any calculus problem into AI and get the answer in 2 seconds. But that's the academic equivalent of using GPS for every single trip — you'll never learn the roads. The exam doesn't have AI. Your professor doesn't care that you can copy an answer. They care that you can solve novel problems using the techniques you've learned.</p>
  <p class="section-text">The right approach: use AI to understand the process, not to skip it. There's a specific technique for this, and it works for everything from algebra to organic chemistry to physics.</p>
</div>

<div class="lesson-section">
  <span class="section-label">The Hint Ladder</span>
  <h2 class="section-title">Start with a nudge. Only escalate if you're truly stuck.</h2>
  <p class="section-text">The hint ladder has four rungs. Start at the top and only climb down when you need to:</p>
  <p class="section-text"><strong>Rung 1 — Concept check:</strong> "What mathematical concept or technique is relevant to this type of problem?" AI tells you it's an integration by parts problem. Now you try it yourself.</p>
  <p class="section-text"><strong>Rung 2 — First step only:</strong> "What's the first step to solve this? Don't solve the whole thing." AI says "Let u = ln(x) and dv = x dx." You take it from there.</p>
  <p class="section-text"><strong>Rung 3 — Guided walkthrough:</strong> "Walk me through the solution step by step, but pause after each step and explain WHY we do this, not just what we do." This builds conceptual understanding — you're learning the reasoning, not memorizing a procedure.</p>
  <p class="section-text"><strong>Rung 4 — Full solution + analysis:</strong> "Solve this completely, then explain the general strategy so I can apply it to similar problems." Use this as a last resort, and immediately try a similar problem on your own to test whether you actually learned the method.</p>
  <div class="tip-box">
    <strong>The follow-up that matters:</strong> After any AI help, ask "Generate 3 similar problems at the same difficulty level." Solve them without AI. If you can do 2 out of 3, you've learned the concept. If not, go back to Rung 3.
  </div>
</div>

<div class="lesson-section">
  <span class="section-label">AI Math Errors</span>
  <h2 class="section-title">AI is surprisingly bad at math. Here's where it fails.</h2>
  <p class="section-text">Large language models don't "do math" — they predict what mathematical notation should look like based on training data. This means they nail standard textbook problems but fail on anything unusual. Common failure modes: multi-step arithmetic errors (especially with fractions and negative signs), misapplying formulas to edge cases, and confidently giving wrong answers to trick questions.</p>
  <p class="section-text">A 2025 benchmark found that GPT-4o solved 87% of standard calculus problems correctly but only 62% of problems with unusual constraints or setups. Claude performed similarly. The takeaway: AI is a good tutor for standard problems but an unreliable calculator for anything complex.</p>
  <p class="section-text"><strong>How to catch errors:</strong> Always check the answer by plugging it back into the original equation. Use estimation to sanity-check (if the area under a curve from 0 to 1 comes out as 47, something's wrong). And for critical homework, verify with Wolfram Alpha or Symbolab, which use actual computation engines, not language prediction.</p>
</div>

<div class="lesson-section">
  <span class="section-label">Science Applications</span>
  <h2 class="section-title">From lab reports to concept mapping, AI accelerates science learning.</h2>
  <p class="section-text">For <strong>lab reports</strong>: AI won't write your report, but it can help you interpret results. "My experiment measured [data]. The expected result was [expected]. Help me brainstorm reasons for the discrepancy." This is exactly the analysis section your professor wants — you just need help generating hypotheses.</p>
  <p class="section-text">For <strong>concept understanding</strong>: Science is full of abstractions. "Explain entropy like I'm explaining it to a friend at dinner" gives you an intuitive grasp that textbook definitions often miss. Then ask "Now give me the rigorous definition and explain how my intuitive understanding maps to it." You get both the vibe and the precision.</p>
  <p class="section-text">For <strong>problem sets</strong>: Physics and chemistry problems often require combining multiple concepts. Ask AI "What concepts does this problem test?" before attempting it. Knowing that a problem requires both conservation of momentum AND energy conservation changes your entire approach.</p>
  <div class="callout">
    <strong>The Wolfram Alpha combo:</strong> Use AI for conceptual explanations and problem-solving strategies. Use Wolfram Alpha for computation and verification. Together, they cover understanding + accuracy — the two things you need.
  </div>
</div>
